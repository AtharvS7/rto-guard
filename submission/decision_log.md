# Decision log

1. **Clean the sample before analysis.** Removed 30 exact duplicate rows, normalized case/whitespace in final statuses and excluded one `IN_TRANSIT` row. Reason: duplicate records distort both baseline rates and validation results; transit is not a final delivery outcome. Three zero-value baskets are retained for outcome-rate reporting but treated as missing model values. Alternative considered: use all 2,018 rows as-is; rejected because it double-counts observations and treats an unresolved order as labelled.
2. **Use a chronological holdout.** Train on April-August 2026 and validate on September 2026. Reason: this better approximates deployment on future orders than a random split. Alternative: random 80/20; rejected because it can leak time-specific courier, source or operational patterns across the split.
3. **Use CatBoost for tabular risk scoring.** It handles mixed numeric/categorical features and non-linear relationships without an expensive LLM call per order. Use engineered address length, locality/landmark flags, prior RTO history, basket features, city/state, courier and acquisition source. Do not pass raw customer name, phone, full address or exact pincode to the classifier.
4. **Keep language understanding narrow.** The optional LLM reviews Hinglish and free-text address usability only, returning structured fields. It never calculates the risk score, estimates unit economics or cancels an order. Deterministic code handles ratios, validation and money because those operations are auditable and reproducible.
5. **Use four risk buckets at 20%, 30% and 70%.** The confirmation assumptions (50% of would-be RTOs prevented; 5% of genuine customers time out) imply a break-even RTO score of about 19.2%; set the ship/confirm boundary at 20% for a small positive cushion. The middle band offers prepaid; the top band is held. Thresholds are editable in `config.yaml` and the app.
6. **Disable auto-cancellation in the pilot.** A 70% model-score threshold produced only three hold-bucket examples in the September holdout. That is not enough evidence for an irreversible action. First-time buyers are protected from auto-cancel; geography alone is never sufficient reason to cancel.
7. **Choose a ₹25 prepaid discount.** At the brief's 28% COD RTO versus 4% prepaid RTO, expected avoided RTO cost is ₹40.66 per prepaid conversion; less ₹25 discount leaves ₹15.66 before gateway fees. Break-even COD RTO is approximately 18.8% with the same assumptions.
8. **Use scenario economics for the dashboard.** Confirmation prevention (50%), confirmation timeout among genuine orders (5%), prepaid conversion (30%) and discount are explicit assumptions in config. Alternatives considered: present projected savings as fact; rejected because no treatment/control outcomes were supplied.
9. **Implement a mock WhatsApp state machine.** Confirmation YES confirms shipment; NO cancels; unexpected text prompts once; no response by 24h is not dispatched and is marked cancelled. For prepaid nudge, YES sends a payment link but does not mark paid; only `PAID` does. NO preserves COD and asks a delivery confirmation; ignored discounts do not cancel the order.
10. **Use SQLite for the local decision log.** It makes the prototype runnable without external infrastructure. Railway deployment is configured with a persistent-volume instruction; production should use a managed database and approved retention rules.
11. **Privacy minimisation.** Do not ship the original sample CSV in Git. The generated validation replay excludes names, phone numbers, raw addresses, exact pincodes and order IDs. The optional LLM path requires an explicit in-app consent checkbox.
12. **Build a transparent demo dashboard.** The 329-order September holdout can be replayed as clearly labelled DEMO data dated yesterday, letting the founder screen be demonstrated without pretending historical orders are live. The WhatsApp simulator uses configurable reminder/deadline labels; its controls manually simulate elapsed time rather than run a background scheduler.
